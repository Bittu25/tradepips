Risk to reward ratio explained with examples

By TradePips · Updated 21 October 2026

The risk to reward ratio compares how much you could lose on a trade with how much you aim to make. It is one of the simplest numbers in trading, and it explains why a trader can be wrong more often than right and still keep their account healthy. This guide shows how to calculate it, what it means for your win rate, and how to set realistic targets from the chart.

What the risk to reward ratio means

  • Risk is the distance from your entry to your stop loss. It is what you lose if the trade fails.
  • Reward is the distance from your entry to your take profit target. It is what you make if the trade works.

The ratio is written as risk first, then reward. A trade that risks 30 pips to aim for 60 pips is 1:2. A trade that risks 30 pips to aim for 30 pips is 1:1.

Reward ÷ Risk = how many times your risk you aim to make

How to calculate it: two worked examples

EUR/USD

You buy at 1.0850. The last higher low puts your stop at 1.0820, which is 30 pips away. The next resistance is at 1.0910, which is 60 pips away.

60 ÷ 30 = 2, so the trade is 1:2

Gold (XAU/USD)

You buy gold at 4,100. Your stop goes at 4,085, which is $15 below. The next swing high is at 4,130, which is $30 above.

$30 ÷ $15 = 2, so the trade is also 1:2

Notice that the ratio uses distances, not money. Your lot size decides how many rupees or dollars those distances are worth, which our guide to calculating lot size explains.

Why the ratio matters more than your win rate

Every ratio has a break even win rate: the share of trades you must win just to finish level, before costs.

Break even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward multiple)

Risk to rewardYou need to win at least
1:150% of trades
1:1.540% of trades
1:233% of trades
1:325% of trades

Here is the arithmetic for 20 trades, each risking $100 at 1:2. If 8 trades win and 12 lose, the wins add $1,600 and the losses cost $1,200. You lost more trades than you won and still finished $400 ahead before spreads and fees. This is only arithmetic, not a forecast: real results depend on how good your trades are, and most retail traders lose money.

The same maths works against you. Risk 50 pips to make 10 pips (1:0.2) and you need to win more than 83% of the time just to break even.

Set targets from the chart, not from a number

A common beginner habit is to pick 1:3 on every trade and place the target three times the stop distance away, wherever that lands. The market does not know your ratio. Instead:

  1. Place your stop where the trade idea is proven wrong. Our guide on how to use a stop loss covers this.
  2. Find the first level where price is likely to react: the next swing high or low, or a clear support or resistance zone.
  3. Measure the ratio between those two real levels.
  4. If the ratio is poor, skip the trade. Many traders want at least 1:1.5 before they enter.

Skipping a trade costs nothing. Taking a trade with a small reward and a large risk costs you over time.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Shrinking the stop to improve the ratio. A stop placed inside normal noise turns a good ratio on paper into frequent small losses.
  • Moving the target further away mid trade because the trade is going well. Decide before you enter.
  • Closing winners early and letting losers run. This quietly turns a 1:2 plan into a 2:1 reality.
  • Ignoring costs. The spread is paid on every trade and matters most on small targets.

Track it in R

Many traders record results as multiples of risk, called R. A full loss is minus 1R and a 1:2 winner is 2R, whatever the lot size. Over a few weeks of journaling this shows clearly whether your planned ratio matches what really happens.
